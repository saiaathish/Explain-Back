Animal cells maintain unequal concentrations of sodium and potassium ions across the plasma membrane. The sodium-potassium pump moves three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions into the cell during each transport cycle. Because three positive charges leave while only two enter, each cycle contributes a small net negative charge inside the cell.

The pump is an active transporter rather than a channel for passive diffusion. Sodium ions are moved against their concentration gradient, and potassium ions are also moved against their concentration gradient, so the transport cannot proceed without an energy source. ATP binds to the pump and transfers a phosphate group to it; phosphorylation changes the pump's shape and exposes the sodium-binding sites to the outside of the cell.

After sodium is released, potassium binds from outside the cell. The phosphate group then leaves the pump, causing it to return to its original shape and release potassium into the cytoplasm. Repeating this cycle maintains the ion gradients that support the resting membrane potential, secondary active transport, and electrical signaling in neurons and muscle cells.
